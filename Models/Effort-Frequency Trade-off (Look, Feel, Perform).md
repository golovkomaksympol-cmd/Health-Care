# Effort–Frequency Trade-off — Look / Feel / Perform (Andy Galpin)

**Effort-proximity and frequency are inversely coupled**, so the goal you name fixes the dose you're allowed to use — and no single dose serves every goal. Entry point for `plan-workout`: no session is designed before the driver is named.

Not a capacity — the trunk capacity itself is `_core/Models/Core Stability.md`; which adaptation axis you're chasing is `_core/Models/Training Adaptations (Galpin 9).md`.

## Mechanism

One causal chain does the work:

**proximity to failure → recovery cost → ceiling on frequency**, while **motor-control adaptation depends on frequency** (distributed practice, not massed).

So effort-proximity and frequency trade against each other. A goal needing near-failure effort is frequency-limited; a goal needing high frequency must stay far from failure. One prescription therefore cannot serve every goal — not because the goals conflict, but because **the doses do**.

Three outcome classes, placed on that trade-off:

| Vector | What you're buying | Effort proximity | Frequency | Fatigue budget | Selection bias |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **LOOK** | muscle size / local volume | **close to failure** | recovery-capped | high | compound + isolation; isolation carries more weight here than elsewhere |
| **PERFORM** | force transfer, bracing, dynamic stability | high **intent**, not to failure | recovery-capped, inside the strength/sport split | high | heavy free-weight compound + anti-movement |
| **FEEL** | positional control, motor competence | **deliberately submaximal** | high, up to daily | near-zero *by design* | controlled / isolated, low load |

The table sets a dose **class** — effort proximity, frequency, fatigue budget. It does not prescribe ratios or rep brackets; those are not part of the model.

**Second mechanism, independent of the first:** *how hard you contract and brace under load* moves the outcome more than *which exercise you chose*. Effort spent hunting novel exercises is misallocated; effort spent on intent inside a known exercise is not.

### The loading ladder

Sequences a pattern from control toward load. Enter at the step you can own with quality, not the one that looks impressive.

| Step | Stage | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Isometric control** — static hold, no load | plank |
| 2 | **Anti-movement + fatigue** — high-rep bodyweight, resist motion | slow dead bugs |
| 3 | **Anti-movement + load** — resist motion against resistance | suitcase carry, DB dead bug |
| 4 | **Movement + fatigue** — dynamic, light, high-rep | cable crunch, contraction-focused |
| 5 | **Movement + load** — heavy dynamic through full ROM | weighted crunch |

Steps 1–3 sit at the FEEL/PERFORM end (control, bracing); 4–5 at the LOOK end (tension, volume). This is a sequencing heuristic for load tolerance and quality — not an injury-prevention protocol.

## Scale of simplicity

The complex object is "hundreds of exercises × load × frequency × individual." This model coarse-grains to **one categorical question — which outcome class is the primary driver? — and returns a dose class, not an exercise.** The simplifying move is refusing to reason about the individual movement: fix effort-proximity, frequency and fatigue budget, and the exercise becomes interchangeable within its pattern (which is why `Exercise Catalog.md` swaps by pattern + adaptation rather than by name).

**Usefulness test:** the payoff exists **only when one primary driver is actually named.** Decline to pick, and the model returns nothing — it relabels the choice instead of collapsing it. It also stops paying off below its scale: it cannot choose between two isolation exercises or set a precise ratio. Use it for the dose class; use the catalog for the movement.

## Cynefin domain

- **Mechanism — Complicated.** Mechanical and derivable: near-failure work costs recovery, recovery caps frequency, control needs distributed practice. Predictable and repeatable.
- **Outcome — Complex.** Whether a block makes *this* person look how they want, or feel better, is emergent — it competes with energy balance, sleep, stress, prior injury, natural history. **Never lend the trade-off's confidence to the outcome.**

## Time horizon (Lyapunov)

- **Dose → adaptation: weeks → a few months.** Predictable; program here.
- **Beyond a block, and for any *outcome* claim at any horizon** → prediction decays. **Stop forecasting, instrument:** re-test each vector on its own metric (LOOK: girth/photos at fixed conditions · PERFORM: 1RM, carry load, jump · FEEL: Sahrmann level, McGill endurance seconds and ratios) on a fixed cadence and steer off measurements.

## Practice

1. **Name the primary driver before selecting anything.** One driver leads per region per block; it sets effort-proximity, frequency and fatigue budget. If it isn't known, establishing it is the first task.
2. **Secondary vectors get a maintenance dose, not a competing one.** FEEL costs almost no fatigue and can run daily alongside LOOK or PERFORM — provided it stays submaximal.
3. **Never take FEEL work near failure.** It trades away the frequency that makes it work. The single most common error this model exists to catch.
4. **Enter the ladder where quality holds.** Earn the loaded steps.
5. **Deepen intent before widening repertoire.** Same exercise, harder contraction and bracing, beats a new exercise. Rotating movements for variety is not progress.
6. **Frequency is a lever for control, not a substitute for effort.** Raising frequency serves motor learning; it does not replace proximity-to-failure or volume when the driver is LOOK.
7. **Diagnose a stalled program as dose-mismatch first:** near-failure work at daily frequency (LOOK dose, FEEL frequency) → accumulating fatigue, no result. Low-load control work once a week (FEEL dose, LOOK frequency) → no adaptation of anything.

## Confidence

- **High** that near-failure effort costs recovery and limits how often a pattern can be repeated with quality.
- **High** that distributed practice beats massed practice for motor-control retention.
- **Medium** that three buckets are the right carve-up — a useful partition, not a measured one.
- **Medium** on the ladder's ordering as a quality/tolerance progression: mechanically sensible, not trialled.

## Falsification

**Prediction if the model holds:** for control/competence at matched total volume, submaximal-and-frequent beats maximal-and-infrequent — because the second option's recovery cost forecloses the frequency that motor learning needs.

**Attempt 1 — is frequency necessary, or is it just volume wearing frequency's clothes?** The strongest attack: for hypertrophy, meta-analyses find training frequency matters little once weekly volume is equated. If that generalises, "frequency is required" collapses into "volume is required" and the trade-off loses its force.
**Result — survives, narrowed to motor learning.** Volume-equivalence does hold for hypertrophy, but not for skill acquisition, where distributed practice reliably beats massed practice for retention. So the frequency arm is a claim about **motor-control/skill adaptation specifically** — the FEEL vector. For LOOK, the frequency reasoning carries no weight and must not be used to justify splitting volume.

**Attempt 2 — does near-failure *low-load* work really cost enough recovery to cap frequency?** A set of dead bugs to failure is not a squat to failure. If the systemic cost is trivial, the prohibition is over-stated.
**Result — partially conceded.** The cost that binds here is **local and task-specific (neural, tissue-level), not systemic.** The prohibition is strongest for patterns loading the spine or a rehabbing tendon, and weakest for genuinely trivial low-load drills — where "near failure" may cost little and the frequency ceiling barely bites.

**Attempt 3 — the direct test, which has not been run.** Volume-matched head-to-head: submaximal control work 5–6×/wk versus near-failure control work 2×/wk, outcome measured as Sahrmann level and McGill endurance. ❓ **The central prohibition is mechanically reasoned and consistent with motor-learning evidence, but not directly demonstrated in this form.** This is the experiment that would settle it.

**Verdict — survives for the FEEL/control vector, narrowed.** The frequency arm is a motor-learning claim rather than a general one; the recovery arm is task-specific rather than systemic. The load-bearing conclusion — name the driver, and don't grind work whose value depends on repetition — holds.

## Boundaries & doubts

- **The three buckets are a heuristic partition, not a measured one.** FEEL is not one of Galpin's 9 adaptations; this model and the 9-axis model carve the space differently and must not be mechanically merged.
- **The frequency argument applies to control/skill, not to hypertrophy.** Do not use it to argue for splitting hypertrophy volume across more sessions.
- **Says nothing about energy balance** — the dominant term for the visible LOOK outcome is body fat, which this model does not address.
- **Weakest in acute pathology.** With disc injury, fracture or radiculopathy, structure dominates and diagnosis outranks any dosing rule.
- ❓ Whether FEEL work's near-zero fatigue cost holds at daily frequency in someone already training hard, or quietly accumulates.
- ❓ For very low-load patterns, whether the effort→recovery→frequency chain binds tightly enough to matter at all.
