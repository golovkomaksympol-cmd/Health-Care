# Core Stability

A **region-specific stability** capacity (the trunk/axial one), sitting in the *Stability / motor-control* layer of the Cook readiness foundation — **below** Skill (`_core/Models/Skill & Technique (Galpin 1).md`). Not a Galpin adaptation, not a muscle, not a movement.

## Mechanism

Core stability = **the ability to control the position/motion of the spine and pelvis, and to transfer force** between upper and lower body. The core's job is mostly to **resist unwanted movement and transmit force**, not to produce big movement. Expressed across:

- **Anti-extension** (resist arching — plank, dead bug)
- **Anti-lateral-flexion** (resist side-bend — side plank, suitcase carry) ← frontal-plane / "adduction-abduction" control
- **Anti-rotation** (resist twist — Pallof press)
- **Rotation control / production** (chop, rotational throws)
- **Bracing / stiffness** for force transfer (McGill)

Unifying principle: **proximal stability for distal mobility** — a stable centre lets the limbs produce force safely ("can't fire a cannon from a canoe").

**"Strength" is a misnomer.** McGill: spinal health tracks core **endurance + motor control + stiffness**, not max strength. Train it as control/endurance, not 1RM.

## Scale of simplicity

"Is my spine/trunk OK under load?" invites an intractable dive into discs, dozens of muscles, and fibre types. The model makes it simple by coarse-graining to a **single trunk-level capacity — control + endurance + stiffness for force transfer** — measured by a movement task (Sahrmann level, McGill endurance seconds), not by any muscle. The simplifying move is dropping "which muscle" for "can the trunk hold neutral and transmit force," which collapses to a handful of tests and the McGill Big 3.

**Usefulness test:** this scale pays off where **control is the limiter** (hypermobile / poor-motor-control profiles). In acute structural pathology (disc, fracture, radiculopathy) structure — not control — dominates, the trunk-capacity scale no longer makes the case simple, and you need imaging/diagnosis instead (see Boundaries).

## Cynefin domain

- **Mechanism** — **Complicated.** Proximal stiffness → distal force transfer, and "resist unwanted motion" are ordered, derivable, and repeatably testable (Sahrmann, McGill endurance). Cause→effect holds via mechanics.
- **Outcome** (injury prevention / pain reduction / performance in the field) — **Complex.** Whether trained core control actually lowers *this* athlete's injury or back-pain risk is emergent: it competes with load management, sleep, technique, prior injury, psychosocial factors. Do not lend the mechanism's confidence to the whole-system outcome.

## Time horizon (Lyapunov)

- Motor-control adaptations from targeted work show over **weeks → a few months** (measurable as Sahrmann level / endurance-test seconds and ratios). That window is predictable.
- Beyond ~a season, the *outcome* chain (does better control → fewer injuries / less pain / faster running) decays into the complex domain → **stop forecasting, instrument:** re-test Sahrmann + McGill ratios periodically, track left/right asymmetry and gait-collapse signs, treat those as tripwires rather than assuming carry-over.

## Practice

- **Test:** **Sahrmann 5-level core test** (maintain neutral lumbopelvic position via abdominal bracing under progressively harder leg loading, levels 0–5 → exposes where control fails). Plus **McGill endurance tests** (side-bridge, flexor, extensor endurance + their ratios).
- **Train:** **McGill Big 3** (curl-up, side bridge, bird dog) as the base; anti-extension / anti-lateral-flexion / anti-rotation progressions; **carries** (suitcase, farmer); **Pallof press**, chops. Neutral spine, quality reps, endurance bias — not heavy 1RM.

## Confidence

- **High** that core = control + endurance + force-transfer (not max strength) and that it's the trunk-specific expression of stability.
- **Medium** on exact test cut-offs and which specific drills are optimal (principle robust, drill specifics debated).

## Falsification

**Attempt:** The strongest counter-case is that "core stability" as a *training paradigm* is oversold. Lederman's *"The myth of core stability"* (2010) argues isolated core/TrA activation has weak transfer. Multiple meta-analyses of low-back pain (e.g. Cochrane; Smith et al. 2014, *BMC Musc Disord*) find **core-stability exercise is no better than general exercise** for pain/function beyond the short term. Injury-prevention trials are mixed and mostly can't isolate a core-specific effect. If those hold, the model's *outcome* claim (train the core specifically → prevent injury / fix pain) is false.

**Result — qualified, core survives.** What fails is the strong, over-marketed claim: *isolated* core drills are not magic and are not superior to general loaded movement for population-level pain/injury outcomes (this is why "outcome = Complex" above). What survives is the mechanical core the model actually asserts: proximal stability enables distal force transfer, and trunk control/endurance/stiffness (not max strength) is a real, testable capacity. Kept because the model is used here as a **foundational control constraint for a specific hypermobile individual** (control is the limiter) — exactly the sub-population where general-population meta-analyses are least informative. Downgrade any implied "this prevents injury" language to "instrument and re-test" (see Time horizon).

## Boundaries & caveats

- **Stability is region-specific** — core is one region; others (ankle/foot, knee, shoulder) become their own concern only when they're a live constraint. Don't build them speculatively.
- Don't conflate with limb strength: a strong squatter can have poor anti-rotation control.
- **Scale/context edges:** the unit is a *movement task*, not a fibre; not limb strength, not a single muscle (TrA/multifidus are contributors, not the model). Weakest as an explanatory model in acute pathology (disc, fracture, radiculopathy) where structure dominates over control.
